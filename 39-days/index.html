39 DAYS

A Not Your Fucking Tea Original

I. THE STORAGE UNIT

People want the lightning bolt. They want the montage — the single dramatic morning where the music swells and the old life falls off your shoulders like a coat you finally decided not to wear. I understand the appeal. I was raised on that story too. The idea that transformation announces itself. That you'll know the exact hour your life changed, because the sky will look different when you step outside.

Nobody tells you it actually looks like a storage unit.

Fluorescent light. A payment that finally cleared. Boxes you haven't opened in longer than you want to say out loud, stacked in a rented room you have been paying, month after month, for the privilege of forgetting about.

That's where this starts. Not in a courtroom. Not in a boardroom. Not in some cinematic confrontation with a man in a good suit. In a ten-by-ten unit, at the end of a day that had already tried to break me twice before noon, doing the least glamorous math of my life: what do I keep, what do I release, and what has been trying to tell me something for months while I was too dysregulated to hear it.

I paid that unit off. And somewhere between closing the account and lying down that night, it landed on me — every piece of chaos this year wasn't a detour from the story. It was the material. I just hadn't organized it yet.

Here's the part I didn't expect.

The unit became the gym.

Not by design. By availability. It was the only floor I had that nobody else was standing on. Ten by ten, concrete, a roll-up door I could pull halfway down so the light came in at the ankles. No mirrors. No music. No membership, no front desk, nobody at a counter deciding whether my account was in good standing before letting me use my own body. Just a rented rectangle full of everything I'd been too tired to look at, and me, on the floor of it, counting.

The symmetry wasn't lost on me. I was doing push-ups next to boxes I hadn't opened. Squatting three feet from paperwork I hadn't filed. Every rep happening inside a room that was, by definition, a monument to postponement.

And the boxes didn't move. I did.

That's not a metaphor I reached for. That's just what happened. The unit stayed exactly as full as it was. My shoulders didn't. That's the first honest thing this year gave me: the difference between a room getting better and a man getting stronger inside it. Only one of those is under my control on any given Tuesday, and I had spent a great deal of time confusing the two.

There's a formula I've been carrying since. Somebody else's handwriting, somebody else's notebook, but it reads like it was written for this year specifically:

QL = (WN × SL)

Quality of Life equals Wins Noticed times Standard of Life.

Quality of life is how I experience it. Standard of life is the bar I hold myself to. And the thing that makes it useful — the thing that makes it more than a poster — is that it's multiplication. Not addition. Addition is forgiving. Addition lets you be sloppy in one column and make it up in the other. Multiplication doesn't negotiate. Hold a perfect standard and notice none of it, you get zero. Notice everything while holding no standard at all, you still get zero. Both terms, or nothing.

I spent most of this year with one of those terms at zero and I couldn't have told you which.

So I started on the floor of the unit. Not a plan. Not a program. A count.

Twenty-five.

Fifty.

Seventy-five.

One hundred.

II. THE DOLLAR EIGHTY

Small story first, because the small ones are where you can actually see yourself.

I'm in a drive-through. I've got points, a balance, a benefit I'm entitled to use. The screen isn't registering it. The kid at the window wants the easy resolution — tap the card, cash or credit, sir, let's move the line along. Behind me there's a lineup. Beside me there's a speaker box. And inside me there's this old, familiar pull, the one that says just comply. Just make the friction go away. It's only a few dollars.

I didn't move.

Does the balance change, I asked. No? Then we're not done.

We tried again. And again. Not because a dollar eighty was going to make or break me. Because I have spent an entire year training myself out of a reflex — the one that says let it go, don't make a scene, don't be the guy holding up the line. That reflex is not a personality trait. That reflex is a discount somebody else gets to apply to me. It is exactly the reflex that let people underpay me, overlook me, route my paperwork to the wrong department, and eventually try to write me out of my own story with a letter they'd already typed.

So: no. Not today. Not over a dollar eighty. Not ever again by default.

We got it down. Small win.

But small wins are how you find out whether the muscle actually works, or whether you were only ever strong inside your own head. You can't test a standard on the big things. The big things come once a year and by then it's too late to find out you fold. You test it on a dollar eighty at a drive-through window at seven in the morning, with a stranger's patience running out in front of you, and you find out exactly what you're made of for about ninety seconds.

Refusing to tap the card is a rep. Same muscle. Different room.

Twenty-five.

Fifty.

Seventy-five.

One hundred.

III. THE SEQUENCE

Here's the sequence, plainly, because plain sequence is the sharpest instrument there is.

On the fourth, I put it on the record. A doctor's note. Documentation covering missed shifts tied to a medication change my physician and I were actively working through. Not a demand. Not an ultimatum. Not even, at that stage, a formal request for anything — we were still working out whether adjusting the timing would let me stay on nights and keep the premium, or whether days would eventually serve the treatment better if it didn't. An open medical conversation, documented, in progress.

By the fifth, the paper had already been written. Not delivered. Written. A decision, dated, sitting in a file, waiting for a room to be delivered in.

They let me work the sixth like nothing had happened. Full shift. No conversation. No indication that the ground under me had been quietly removed the day before while I was still standing on it.

And on the morning of the seventh, they handed me a letter dated two days earlier, telling me I wasn't the right fit.

I didn't understand what that sequence meant while I was standing inside it. You live your life forward and you only understand it backward — that's not a platitude, that's just how timestamps work. It took distance to see the thing that was obvious the whole time: the decision was made the day after the medical documentation entered the file. Not the day I stopped performing. Not the day the tact-time numbers moved. The day after.

Fit isn't dated. Reactions are.

And then there's the part they left hanging. The background check — the report they required as a condition of employment, the one I paid for out of pocket, fingerprinted for, waited on — was still pending. Still open. On the tenth, three days after they'd already put me out the door, HR was still emailing me chasing its arrival.

Sit with the shape of that. They terminated the file before they finished building it. They decided I wasn't a fit using a folder they hadn't closed. They were still requesting documents for an employee they'd already removed.

I'd paid out of pocket for the fingerprinting. Covered the onboarding costs. Showed up ready to prove not just that I could complete the work, but that I could complete it on time, on a schedule my doctor and I had already agreed to. I wasn't asking for less accountability. I was asking for the room to meet it my way.

They answered that with a pre-dated letter and my own tools finding their way into a lost-and-found bin before I did. Cute. Maybe it was carelessness. Maybe it was something with a sense of humour about itself. Either way, I noticed.

I always notice. That's the whole point of this essay.

I didn't walk out of that building with nothing. I walked out holding the one thing they forgot people like me know how to read.

Timestamps. Sequences. The order things actually happened in, as opposed to the order somebody would prefer to explain them in.

A sequence doesn't lie. Even when everyone standing inside it insists it doesn't mean a thing.

Twenty-five.

Fifty.

Seventy-five.

One hundred.

IV. SAME BODY, DIFFERENT HAND

They think the needle is the story.

It never was.

I kept the hand. I kept the hour. I kept the practiced, unremarkable calm of a man who has done this before and will do it again tomorrow and will not be making an announcement about it either time. What I changed was what it served.

One version cost me days. I'd go under and come back up and the calendar would be lying about how much time had gone missing. Days I can't itemize. Days that never got logged anywhere because nobody was counting, least of all me. That version had a standard too — it was rigorous, it was punctual, it never missed. It just held me to a bar set by something that wasn't me.

This version costs me twenty minutes. Prescribed, paid for, on schedule, and then the day comes back to me whole. Same hand. Same hour. Same quiet before the house wakes up.

Nobody claps for that.

There is no chip, no ceremony, no anniversary card for a man who kept the discipline and quietly swapped out the master it answered to. The ritual looks identical from the outside. That's precisely why it doesn't get counted — the world scores intention by how different it looks, and this doesn't look like anything at all.

So I score it myself. That's the WN term. That's why the sheet exists.

Because here's what I learned the hard way about the noticing: I cannot be trusted to do it in my head. Dysregulated, my memory is a negotiator. It shaves. It rounds up. It tells me eighty-four was basically a hundred and that yesterday was basically today. And a standard you can't verify isn't a standard, it's a mood.

But there's a second thing, and it took me until nearly the end of this to see it, and it is the most useful thing in the essay, so I'm going to say it slowly.

Dysregulated, I don't just fail to notice. I fail to retrieve.

The information is already there. That's the part that nearly took me out. It isn't that I didn't have the document, or hadn't lived the day, or couldn't have told you the date if somebody put a gun to it. It's that the file was in the room and I could not open the drawer. My own record, sitting in my own possession, and the retrieval mechanism was offline because the body underneath it was running on four hours and no food and a nervous system that had been on alert since March.

You cannot think your way out of that. I tried. I tried for months. I sat very still and concentrated very hard on a problem whose solution I already owned, and got nothing, and concluded — because this is what the dysregulated mind concludes — that I was stupid, and slow, and probably deserved the outcome.

What actually worked was standing up.

Every genuine breakthrough this year arrived after the reps, not before them. Not because exercise is inspiring. Because the count regulates the system, and a regulated system can open its own drawers. Movement wasn't the reward for figuring it out. Movement was the precondition for being able to.

That's the mechanism underneath the formula. Wins Noticed isn't a gratitude practice. It's a retrieval protocol. And the body is the key you have to turn before the cabinet opens at all.

Here's what I didn't say yet, because it needed the rest of this said first: for a long stretch, I wasn't turning that key clean. I had two other keys I'd been using instead — a stimulant to force the ignition, and a different kind of arousal to force it when the stimulant wasn't the tool at hand. Both did the same job. Both got me to twenty-five, fifty, seventy-five, one hundred, on days I otherwise wouldn't have moved. And both meant I never actually found out what the engine could do running clean, because I never once let it try.

This stretch, I did. No stimulant beyond what a doctor prescribes, no chemical push to get the body upright. No arousal-as-ignition either — over a week without reaching for it, which doesn't sound like much written down and was, in practice, the first honest baseline read I've taken all year. Turns out the body starts anyway. Slower. Nothing dramatic. It just gets up, because getting up is a decision now instead of a chemical event.

Nothing isn't available to a living body. There is always a rep the body can produce without a catalyst. The shower became the rep, on the days that was all there was. Not because the shower is impressive. Because it was honest, and honest was the entire experiment.

So it gets written. Left side or right side. Start time, end time. The count, honest, including the days the count came up short — especially those days, because eighty-four written down is data and eighty-four recorded as a hundred is the old version clearing its throat.

I'm not going to tell you I got clean and everything got bright. That's the montage again, and I already told you what I think of the montage.

What I'll tell you is smaller and truer: I stopped subtracting.

One hundred and nine.

V. THE FILE

While all of that was running, something slower and colder was running underneath it.

An application to a provincial support program. Submitted in January. Still unresolved by May.

In April they threatened to close the file if I didn't respond fast enough. I responded — same week, documented. The application moved forward, briefly, and then landed on a denial. Two of the three criteria were granted outright. The third failed, and the third was permanence: whether the thing I live with is the kind of thing that lasts.

That's the whole hinge. Not whether it's real. Not whether it's severe. Whether it's permanent.

So I submitted a request for reconsideration with updated documentation.

They routed it to appeals.

Which is a different process entirely — one that can generally only weigh what's already sitting in the file, not new information. Meaning the letter I'd just obtained, the one that answered their stated reason for denial directly, might never have been read by anyone with the authority to act on it.

I caught it. Said so in writing, immediately, with dates. Resubmitted correctly.

And still, weeks on, I'm the one holding the thread — tracking a bureaucracy that seems capable of losing its own paperwork faster than I can produce it, in a process where the deadline runs against me and the burden of catching their error lands on me.

Notice the shape. It's the same shape as the letter dated the fifth.

An institution makes a filing error. The cost of that error is transferred, silently, to the person with the least slack in their life to absorb it. Nobody's malicious. Nobody's even really paying attention. That's what makes it work.

Now here's the part I want on the record, because it's the reason Section IV exists.

I had the answer to their question in my pocket the entire time.

There is a federal determination — a different arm of the same government, a different desk, a different letterhead — that already applied the exact test the provincial panel says it can't resolve. Its standard is severe and prolonged. Prolonged, defined: has lasted or is expected to last a continuous extended period. That is a permanence finding. Adjudicated. Dated. Federal. Already granted, with money attached to it, by people who do not hand that out for things they expect to clear up.

One arm of the government certified the duration. Another arm denied support on the grounds that the duration wasn't established.

I didn't create that contradiction. They did. I just have to be regulated enough to point at it.

And I want to be honest about how long that took, because it's the whole thesis: I did not find that document by thinking harder. I found it on a day when I was sore, under-fuelled, and did not want to do anything — and did the squats anyway, and the walk anyway, and about forty minutes after that the drawer opened and there it was. In my possession the entire time. Filed under a heading my own nervous system had been unable to reach for four months.

That's not inspiration. That's mechanics.

An institution counts on you being too depleted to remember what you already hold. That is not a conspiracy. It's just a system that works better when the person inside it is tired — and a system that works better when you're tired will never once suggest that you rest.

I mention it not because it's the same story as the plant. It isn't. I mention it because it's the same skill: reading a system that was never built to make itself legible to the person inside it. Tracking dates the institution is counting on you not to track. Staying earnest without staying naive.

That distinction cost me a year to learn. Earnestness offered without discernment isn't virtue. It's just fuel for somebody else's process.

Twenty-five.

Fifty.

Seventy-five.

One hundred.

VI. THE SIGNAL

There's a habit I've had since I was a kid that I never had a name for until this year, and it turns out it's not a party trick. It's data.

I write down what I notice before I know why I noticed it. A dream. A stray thought that shows up sideways, unrelated to whatever else is going on. I used to log these because I liked the pattern of it, the way a kid keeps a rock collection. This year I started checking them against the calendar.

Two entries, close together, both worth putting on the record. One: a dream about being let go from a job, logged on the second of August. The actual termination landed on the seventh. Five days. Two: a passing thought about a tool going missing, also on the second. A drill went missing at work on the third. One day.

I want to be precise about what I'm claiming, because it would be easy to overclaim and I'd rather be right than interesting. I'm not saying the writing caused the outcome. I'm not building a prophecy. What I'm saying is narrower and, I think, more useful: the gap between the noticing and the event is itself worth tracking, and I don't yet have a theory for why the gap exists at all. I just have the dates, and the dates are real, and pretending I don't notice the pattern would be its own kind of dishonesty.

So now there are two logs running instead of one. The retrospective one, mining what I already said or wrote before I knew what it meant. And a prospective one, starting now — writing down what I notice as I notice it, and clocking the days until, if ever, it shows up in the world. Comparing the two isn't superstition. It's just refusing to throw away a data source because I can't yet explain the mechanism.

Here's the part that reframed the dream itself, once I sat with it longer. I don't think it was telling me the job was doomed. I think it was telling me, in the only channel my daytime attention couldn't argue with or edit, that my priorities were already organized somewhere else — and that if I didn't leave on my own terms, the exit would happen anyway, louder and worse and not on my schedule. I didn't listen. The exit came exactly like that.

Sleep doesn't negotiate with the version of me that's trying to manage optics. That's what makes it worth logging. Everything else I produce during the day passes through a filter built to keep me looking fine. Sleep doesn't pass through that filter. Whatever gets written down from it is closer to unedited than anything I generate on purpose.

There's a mechanism for this that isn't mystical at all — the reticular activating system, the part of the brain that decides, out of everything available, what actually gets through to conscious attention. It's retunable. What you name, what you write down, what you keep asking it to flag — that's what it starts surfacing. The daily sheet isn't separate from this. It's the same mechanism, aimed on purpose instead of running on whatever the day happens to throw at it.

I don't know yet what the gap means. I know it's there, and I know ignoring it would be the old version of me, the one that couldn't open his own drawer.

Twenty-five.

Fifty.

Seventy-five.

One hundred.

VII. BORING MYTHIC

I want to be honest about the year, not just the parts that flatter me.

I came back from a sabbatical that was supposed to be restoration and turned into erosion. Fifty pounds heavier. An arrangement that fell apart before it became anything. A family procurement project I got quietly excluded from because my title didn't say engineer — even while the thing moved four million dollars through hands that apparently didn't require the credential I was told I was missing. I came home to a house with a foreclosure sign on the lawn and brothers who had already moved on without me in the room.

Somewhere in there I stopped keeping the horizon in view. I was doing the math faster and faster and forgetting what the math was for. I slept somewhere I didn't plan to sleep. I lost my keys and panicked at people who hadn't earned it. I answered emails I should have let sit overnight. I conceded to pressure from an employer who contacted me across three separate platforms until earnestness felt like the only available response.

That's the part that doesn't go in the highlight reel, because it isn't heroic. It's just true. And I've decided true is more useful to me than heroic from here forward.

Which brings me to what the version of myself I'm reaching for actually looks like. I used to think it would look like a film. Third act. Scored, lit, witnessed.

What it actually looks like is this: clocking my hours honestly. Watching my own output instead of my own excuses. Sitting with an upset long enough to extract what it's telling me instead of either smothering it or letting it drive. Training like this is the marathon it actually is, and not a single race with a finish line I sprint at and then rest behind forever.

There's a writer — you probably know the book — who puts it plainly: he's been hurt so many times that nothing can hurt him now. Not because the hurt stopped landing. Because he stopped being surprised by it. Stopped requiring it not to happen in order to keep moving.

That's the register I'm building toward. Not invincibility. Just the refusal to be derailed by a thing merely because it's painful.

That's the boring mythic. Slow-clapping for yourself in an empty room because you did the unremarkable thing on time again, with nobody there to witness it but the version of you keeping count.

And the empty room, most days, is ten by ten and full of boxes.

Fifty.

VIII. WHO'S AUTHORING THIS

Somewhere in the middle of all of it I asked myself the only question that actually mattered.

Who is offering this reality?

For a stretch, the honest answer was: not me. The drive-through was authoring it. The letter dated the fifth was authoring it. A denial from a case worker who had never met me was authoring it. Every institution capable of generating a form faster than I could generate a response was, functionally, writing my week for me.

I'm done outsourcing the pen.

This is the part where I stop asking someone else to walk me through the fundamentals one tutorial at a time and start timing myself against my own best attempt instead of somebody else's patience. Where reflection stays mine to do — with company when I want it, never as a substitute for doing my own authorship.

Nobody hands me a pre-dated letter and calls it my ending. Nobody misfiles my paperwork and calls it my failure to qualify.

I read timestamps. I read sequences. That is the one skill this year could not take off me, and God knows it tried.

My sister called this year, first real conversation we'd had since May, and near the bottom of it she told me something I needed to hear and didn't want to: that what I was going through was affecting everyone around me, not just me. That's part of what got me back into a room with a paycheck attached. Peace, she said, is just the absence of chaos — not a feeling you chase, a condition you stop generating. I've been turning that over since. It tracks with the formula. Peace grows the same way the score does: accept the wins, hold the standard, and stop manufacturing the next fire.

She also told me about a friend of hers, mid-shift somewhere, about to get shorted fifteen hours of pay by a lead who figured nobody would check. My sister said thinking about how I push back on paperwork, on discounts I'm not owed, on letters dated two days before they should be — that's what gave her friend the nerve to record her own supervisor and demand the hours get reviewed.

I didn't do that. I want to be precise about that too. She did that, on her own, because she decided she was allowed to. But I'll take the ripple. The friction doesn't stay in the room where it started. It moves. Somebody two names removed from me is holding her employer to a standard because a story about a dollar eighty made its way to her secondhand. That's authorship too — the part that doesn't come back to you signed.

Twenty-five.

Fifty.

IX. THE VERDICT

Let me say the thing I've spent this whole essay walking toward.

On the fourth, I handed them a doctor's note.

On the fifth, they wrote my termination letter.

They didn't need twenty-four hours to decide I wasn't a fit. They needed twenty-four hours to decide what to call it.

And the file they used to make that decision was still open. Still pending. HR was chasing its arrival on the tenth, three days after I was already gone. They terminated the file before they'd finished building it.

So which is it.

Was I let go for tact time?

Or was I let go the day after they found out why I'd been missing it?

They can't answer that either way without answering it.

Here's the same shape, one desk over. A panel says the duration isn't established. A federal determination establishing the duration already exists, granted, dated, in my possession. They aren't in conflict with me. They're in conflict with each other, and I'm just the man holding both letters.

I didn't get fired. I got handed a confession — dated, signed, and timestamped by people who assumed nobody in my position reads that closely.

Some sequences don't survive being looked at.

This one didn't survive discovery.

X. DAY 39

I don't know exactly what day thirty-nine looks like from here. That's not modesty. That's the entire architecture. I lived the first thirty-eight forward and I'm only now beginning to understand them backward — in the order they mean something, rather than the order they happened to hurt.

Here's what I do know.

The storage unit is paid off, and it has a clean patch of floor in it now, roughly the size of a man lying down. The timeline is documented. The reconsideration is resubmitted, to the correct department, with the right language attached, and the federal finding is going in the record where it belonged from the start. The letter to the plant is written, factual, and dated. The dollar eighty got resolved because I refused to let it not be.

And every piece of this year that felt like derailment is starting to look, from this side of it, like exactly what it was: evidence. Collected in real time by a man who didn't yet know what case he was building.

I know now.

The formula holds. Quality of life is the experience. Standard of life is the bar. And the multiplication sign in between them is the reason none of this works if you only do half — the reason a man can hold a perfect standard in an empty room and still come out at zero if he never once writes down that he held it.

But the reps aren't there to earn the noticing. The reps are there to make the noticing possible. I don't move because I've figured it out. I move so that I can.

So I write it down. Every day. Left or right. Start and end. The count, honest.

One hundred push.

One hundred pull.

One hundred squats.

And a hundred and nine sit-ups — a hundred for the year, and nine for the days I lost and cannot itemize, the ones that went missing off the calendar while somebody other than me was holding the pen.

Nine extra, every single day, until the ledger reads even.

That's not penance. That's interest.

I didn't get clean. I got even.

Thirty-nine days. Same hell, different levels. I'll see you on the other side of it — with the paperwork, and the proof, and the pen still in my hand.

— Savant
